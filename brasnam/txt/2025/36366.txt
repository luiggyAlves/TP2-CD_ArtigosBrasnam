Abstract. Urban violence remains a challenge for cities, requiring innovative
approaches to provide actionable insights for authorities. This study presents
VISAGE, a framework designed to detect and classify urban violence in social
media textual data using an extension of the Quantification of Violence Scale
(QoVS). Ten Machine learning models, including BERT and Random Forest,
were evaluated on Twitter datasets from violent events in Brazil, with BERT
achieving an F1-score of 0.86. Results demonstrate the feasibility of automat-
ing violence assessment from text. Limitations like dataset imbalance and label-
ing process are discussed with future work targeting real-time and multimodal
analysis.
1. Introduction
Violence has been part of mankind’s experience, and it is considered a public epidemic
health problem [Pedersen 2002]. Violence is the cause of illness, disabilities and several
social consequences, beside deaths. In 2021, the number of victims of homicide was
464.000 world, and in Americas this number was close to 176.000 [Publications 2023].
Such statistics attest to the large social and economic costs of violence.
In an urban context, violence has also been a challenging trend. Cities need to
understand violence records using different sources of data in order to understand its im-
pacts and what are the necessary actions in terms of public policies. In this context,
social media is an important crowdsource mechanism useful to extract data about vio-
lence [Gresenz et al. 2023]. Indeed, people use social media to report different types of
violence, that they suffer or witness. For example, the former Twitter — now known as X
1 — has been known as a platform for reporting different types of violence like gang vi-
olence [Patton et al. 2017] and police brutality [Hawkins 2023]. In Brazil, particularly in

Rio de Janeiro, platforms like Fogo Cruzado 2 and OTT-RJ3 have used Twitter to crowd-
source and disseminate real-time information about armed violence and urban conflicts.
There are different forms of violence(sexual, domestic or verbal violence) which
can be self-inflicted or collective [Krug et al. 2002]. Due to its wide-ranging meaning,
this work will focous on urban violence. This can be defined as any form of violence
occurring in cities and suburbs. It may involve delinquent acts such as theft, physical
assault, harassment and vandalism [Belghit 2024].
According to [Tyrer et al. 2007] planning, intent, and violence’s outcome — or
consequence — are three key dimensions that influences the characterization of any vio-
lent incident. Based upon these factors, a violence scale - named QOVS (Quantity of Vi-
olence Scale) - was proposed in [Tyrer et al. 2007]. Such scale provides objective metrics
to quantify and then assess violence. The QOVS can drive a wide number of applications
to aide and protect citizens using social media as a source of violence report in urban
spaces. To the best of our knowledge, there is no work that sought to classify violence in
levels with a computational solution using the QOVS scale.
Considering the complexity and multidimensionality of violence, as well its grow-
ing manifestation in urban contexts and its representation on social media platforms, this
work proposes to investigate the following research question: How is it possible to de-
velop a computational approach to identify and quantify levels of violence from data
extracted from social media data?
This paper presents VISAGE (an approach for detecting and measuring levels of
Violence expressed In Social media messages). VISAGE is a computational solution that
analyzes violence expressed in social media in order to classify them into a violence scale.
We have adapted a well-known violence assessment scale [Tyrer et al. 2007] in order to
be able of to detect violence reports in social media and to assess the level of violence
reported. The relationship between violent reports and violent real episodes are intent,
but in future works.
In this paper, we present as contribution: (i) a QOVS adaptation to the compu-
tational scenario and (ii) a computational approach to identify violence reports in social
media and their violence levels. The experiments performed had a goal to assess the
feasibility of the proposal.
This paper is organized as follows: (i) section 2 presents the related works, (ii)
section 3 presents the QOVS adaptation and the computational scale to define the signifi-
cance of violence, (iii) section 4 presents the method used for conducting the experiment,
(iv) section 5 describes the set of experiment made to evaluate VISAGE , (v) section 6
presents the results, (vi) section 7 presents the discussion and limitations and finally (vii)
section 8 draws some conclusions and future works.
2. Related works
Violence is a subjective topic [Peixoto et al. 2021] . Its concept may vary according to
the context it is used. For example, a scene may be considered violent by a person, while
others can refer to it as non violent. In order to better understand what is violence, and

how other researches have been handling the challenging to identify violence through
computational approaches, a review of related works was made.
Some authors focused on identifying violence by using video classification as a
tool to detect violence. At this context, [Kozhamkulova et al. 2023], processed videos
by using MoveNET to identify the joints of the human body, using the positions of each
one as parameters for a neural network to recognize bullying. Besides [Pujol et al. 2020]
have used visual features in order to calculate the velocity and orientation of movements
to classify them in violent or non-violent actions. Both approaches have got promising
results for violence detection. The differences between the studies and the present work
are: they have focused on visual features while VISAGE’s focus on text data from so-
cial media; VISAGE’s has a urban violence context as a subject of study, while they
focus on identify physical bullying and other physical violent actions in videos. Still in
the context of computational approaches to detect violence, works like Ponce-León et al.
[Ponce-León and López-Nava ] use image classification models to generate a summary
of a image, which is used together with text recognition of the tweet the image originates
to determine if it is violent or not, achieving better results than the text of the tweet by
itself. Image classification is also way faster than video classification, allowing for its
application in real-time social , provided enough processing power [Peixoto et al. 2021].
However, VISAGE’s scope is limited only to textual data due to the nature of its theoreti-
cal method — QOVS designed to classify violence based only in text reports.
Other studies have used deep learning as a tool to detect other types of vio-
lence. [Toktarova et al. 2023] compared the ROC values of deep learning models against
traditional approaches and showed an improvement in detection of hate speech on so-
cial media. The main difference to this paper is the violence as subject of analysis.
[Toktarova et al. 2023] focused in using deep learning to identify hate of speech, while
this study is performing a comparison of machine learning and deep learning methods,
but to identify violence related to urban context.
A similar approach was explored by [Ponce-León and López-Nava ] by using
Large Language Models as a tool to support the process of identify violence. They used
the GPT-3 model to balance the number of data points in each class by creating data for
the classes that were a minority, resulting in a higher F1-score than using the original
dataset.
Although data augmentation by prompt engineering can be used in datasets with
numerous entries or non-extreme data imbalance, that is not the case for this study. Be-
sides not having an adequate amount of data, running the risk of the artificial entries
overshadowing the real ones. In this study, the classes were also unbalanced. To handle
it, the number of entries for each class was obtained, and the nearest integer number was
defined to scale the other unbalanced classes.
3. VISAGE
The main goal of this paper is to present a computational approach to identify violence
reports in social media and then determine the violence levels in such reports. For this,
the Quantification of Violence Scale was adapted to allow urban violence reports to be
objectively classified within a scale, resulting in the ability to measure the seriousness of
that situation. Therefore, this section describes both the QOVS adaptations and presents

the method to use it.
3.1. Extending the quantification of Violence Scale
The Quantification of Violence Scale is a method developed by [Tyrer et al. 2007] with
the aim to record and assessing episodes of severe violence. The original version scores
planning range from 1 to 6 (for the authors, all violent episode has a minimum planning
as an impulsive act) and from 0 to 5 for both intent and consequence (where 0 is no
intent or consequence and 5 is the maximum score for both). At the VISAGE adaption,
the planning range scores from 0 to 5 as the intent and consequence dimensions. This
decision was made in order to score a message with 0 always it does not express any
violence. VISAGE considers scenarios where violent episodes would be unknown, and
it has to detect the violence report. For example, in a scenario like a big event (as a
championship final soccer game) so many messages are published in social media about
the event. If violent episodes occur in the stadium leveraging posts about the violence, we
should differentiate reports between expressing or not violence.
In Table 1 is presented the range of scores for each violence dimension and the
description about how rating a report with a score per dimension. As described above, the
planning could score with 0 when no planing was identified. The identification of “some
planning” will score 1 to this dimension. The maximum scores for planning, intent and
consequence are for activities which contain ’thorough planning’ (such as kidnapping),
’intent to murder’ (want to kill someone is considered the worst intention) and deaths (the
worst consequence of violence), respectively. The final score of a violent act is the sum
of the planning, intent and consequence scores.
3.2. The Computing Scale of Violence Assessment (CSoVA)
To simplify the classification process, the violence events must be reported within classes.
The main idea is that these classes would function as the scale’s levels, where the first po-
sition encompasses situations of no violence identified and the last one very high violence
level. To that end, five classes were defined observing specifics ranges of final scores
(according to [Tyrer et al. 2007]) which are presented in Table 1. It is important to high-
light that the violence is calculated based upon how much a social media violent report
is scored. As aforementioned, violent episodes may motivate citizens’ report in social
media. We want to evaluate the violence using this report.
Very High Violence Level (VHVL) - [9, 15] This level includes all items that
beyond putting people’s lives at risk has resulted in drastic consequences. In order to
achieve this level of seriousness an event must have a score at least 9 in the adapted QOVS
final score (of a maximum of 15). This punctuation interval has been defined based on the
description of the QOV-Matched Version which defines a cut-off since 9 to define severe
violence.
High Violence Level (HVL) - [7,8] This class encompasses all events that have
high level of planning, intention and consequence. However there are similarities be-
tween MVL and HVL, the major difference is in the fact that in HVL there is no fatal
consequence. The small range of punctuation is due to the fact of this subtle similarity.
Medium Violence Level (MVL) - [4,6] The MVL class is the one that includes
low-severity situations, but in its minimum score at least one of the dimensions - planning,

4. Method
Machine Learning is a technique which enables computers to learn from data, identify-
ing patterns and taking decisions with minimum human intervention [Gresenz et al. 2023,
Llauradó et al. 2023, Mussiraliyeva et al. 2023]. In this paper, the goal is to automatically
identify violence in social media messages and also to classify the level of violence ac-
cording to the QOVS extension. With the concern that the results of this experiment are
models that classify violence, and this is a safety-critical activity, this paper has followed

the steps defined at the Machine Learning lifecycle.
Figure 1. The machine learning lifecycle adapted from [Ashmore et al. 2019]
As shown in the Figure 1, the machine learning lifecycle is a process composed
of four stages. Data management, Model Learning and Model Verification comprise the
activities responsible for building the machine learning model itself. The fourth stage,
Model Deployment, comprises the activity related to the deployment of ML model within
an operational system. However, the deployment is outside the scope of the experiment.
The first stage, Data Management, involves preprocessing tasks to make data
ready for training and evaluation. Text originated from social media can be noisy, which
requires strategies for normalization, removal of irrelevant elements, and label consis-
tency.
The second stage, Model Learning, encompasses the selection and training of
machine learning models. Given the complexity of natural language and the nuances
of violent discourse, this study has experimented with various architectures, including
traditional classifiers and deep learning models.
In the third stage, Model Verification, we addressed the critical need for reliabil-
ity and safety in the model’s predictions. This phase included formal verification meth-
ods—such as checking that the model satisfies predefined behavioral requirements—and
test-based approaches, which assessed model responses to controlled input scenarios.
Finally, while Model Deployment was not performed in this study, we acknowl-
edge its importance for future work. Considerations regarding model integration, moni-
toring, and periodic updating are better described in section number 8.
5. Experiment
This section describes the experiments performed in order to demonstrate the use of VIS-
AGE. We have selected Twitter as the data source. The Twitter API makes easy to retrieve
data, so we use it to build our datasets. A M2 max mac studio with base configurations
and a M1 macbook air with base configurations were used under macOS Sequoia version
15.3.
5.1. Data management
The dataset management stage at machine learning lifecycle, is responsible by collect-
ing, organizing and preparing the data which will be used to train and test the model

[Ashmore et al. 2019]. In this paper, four datasets were collected in order to train the
model used to implement de proposed scale. The datasets are: clashes in Rocinha, brazil-
ian 2013’s protests, protest of police relative ES, Brazil and crime reports.
The first dataset covers tweets about police activity in Rocinha, a Rio de Janeiro’s
neighborhood, during a gang clash from September 13-21, 2017. The posts were searched
through the use of hashtags such as #PMERJ, #Rocinha, and #TirosRJ. These tags are
related to violence in this area.
The second dataset has as background context the 2013 protests in Brazil, captur-
ing posts from Twitter from June 24 to June 30, 2013. This period was marked by violent
protests reported in news media 4. This dataset includes posts related to general topics,
not only violent reports, as detailed by [França et al. 2017].
The third dataset is formed by posts related to the strike of military police from
Espírito Santo, Brazil in February 2017. With the streets without policemen, violent
episodes occurred, with 80 deaths and widespread robberies. Tweets from February 03
to February 09, 2017, were collected using hashtags like like ”#EspiritoSanto”, #espiri-
tosanto, and ”#pmes.”
The fourth has messages from well-known crime-reporting profiles in Rio de
Janeiro (@fogocruzadoapp, @AlertaAssaltoRj, @PMERJ). The tweets were collected
from June 01 up to July 25, 2017. This period reflects a moment of escalating violence in
Rio, culminating in a military intervention, as noted in [Phillips 2018].

Once collected, approximately 500 tweets were randomly selected from each
dataset. Therefore, four sampled datasets were built and proceeded to be labeled by
humans. Two undergraduate students proficient in Brazilian Portuguese labeled them.
They assessed planning, intent and consequence. To do that, they asked themselves the
of planing, intent and consequence and then answered according to the Table 1. The final
violence level was obtained by the sum of scores. A label was assigned to each tweet mes-
sage obeying the CSoVA (Table 2) and the final score it received. These labeled datasets
have their duplicate texts removed during division, reducing the total number of entries
down to 1758. They are shown in Figure 3.
After, the datasets were preprocessed through two main tasks: division and up-
sampled division. Division processes the data and divides it in training and test subsets.
At the division, duplicates entries were deleted, then all the texts are normalized using
the Enelvo normalizer 5 which is used to sanitize the text, removing punctuations, emojis
and other special characters. The normalizer also removes word suffixes, thus reducing
inflected words. Stop words were removed using the NLTK Portuguese stop words list
[Steven Bird et al. 2009]. The text is vectorized using the TF-IDF method and the data
is split in training and test. This method was selected due to better comprehending the
importance of rare words by considering their frequency within the text, showing better
accuracy for classification of hate speech than Count Vectorizer [Suryaningrum 2023] and
being widely used for text classification. After training the non upsampled models, the
dataset is upsampled and select models are trained with the new data.
5.2. Model Learning
In order to validate the proposed scale, this study performed a comparison between six
machine learning algorithms. The chosen algorithms for this task were: BERT classi-
fier, Naive Bayes classifier, Ada Boost classifier, Forest Model, Tree Model and Dummy
Model. Since there are 5 distinct classes (levels of violence) it was adopted a one-vs-all
multiclass classification strategy. Each model was trained considering the following divi-
sion: 70% of the samples were designated for the training task and 30% to the evaluation
of the model. Also, each model was trained against the normal version of the dataset and
the upsampled version in order to compare the performance of each model in an unbal-
anced and balanced scenario, except the BERT, Dummy and Ada Boost model.
To implement the algorithms and its dependencies, the Scikit-learn 6 and trans-
formers 7 toolkits were used. Also, a framework was implemented in order to ensure
the consistency in training and performance assessment. Basically, the work was done
using 3 main components: Model Processing, Data Processing, Metrics Processing and
BERT Processing. The Data Processing component is responsible for every preprocess-
ing task, such as removing the stopwords or vectorize the text with TD-IDF method on all
models except BERT - that uses its own tokenizer. The Model Processing component is
responsible for actually training and returning instances of each trained model. The Met-
rics Processing component is the responsible for performing analyses of a trained model
producing graphs and other manners to check metrics related to a model. The BERT

The Dummy classifier is an artifact that makes predictions that ignore the input
features according to [Pedregosa et al. 2011]. This classifier purpose is to work as a sim-
ple baseline to compare against other, more complex classifiers. The strategy used in this
study was to make the instances of dummy classifier always returning the most frequent
class in the training data and classifies all test input as this class.
The BERT model uses a transformers based architecture with bidirectional en-
coding, allowing for great accuracy and fine tuning of pre-trained models with only one
additional output layer [Devlin et al. 2019]. For this study, a BERTimbau pre-trained
model [Souza et al. 2020] was fine-tuned using the trainer 8 class.
The Adaboost model is a classification model based on the concept of a forest.
This classifier uses fixed-size trees (one node with two leaves each) that makes use of
boosting — a training technique with iterations that assigns more weight to the classes
the model misclassified previously. In this study, the classifier was trained 100 times,
increasing the number of trees from 1 to 100. After the best model is selected. The output
includes the number of trees for the best model obtained in the iterations.
As the Adaboost model, the forest classifier is also based on forests with variable-
sized trees. In our study the classifier was trained with 1500 interactions and the number
of trees was increased from 1 to 50 and maximum depth from 1 to 30. The best settings
as the number of trees and the maximum depth is returned as an output in the ending of
Forest model.
The last tree based classifier used in this study was the decision tree classifier
implemented by the sklearn library. This is also a variable-sized tree model. The number
of iterations set for the decision tree were 100. The maximum depth hyperparameter was
from 1 to 100.
The Naive Bayes methods are a set of supervised learning based on applying
Bayes’ theorem with the assumption of conditional independence between every pair
of features given the value of the class variable [Pedregosa et al. 2011].For this study,
it was selected Gaussian implementation of the Naive Bayes. Besides being based on the
probabilistic approach, the Gaussian approaches assume that each class follows a normal
distribution. The hyperparameter used were: prior probabilities of classes equals ”None”,
and portion of the largest variance of all features equals 1e-9.

7. Discussion
In this experiment, it was performed the evaluation of the proposed adaptation of the
QOVS scale in the task of classifying data originated from social media. To do these, 4
different datasets were collected from X (Twitter) social media, observing tags that refer
to contexts which were related to urban violence. After, a subset of this data was manually
labeled following the proposed QOVS adaption.
The experiment suggests that tree-based models have better performance when
compared to the other approaches. The best models were the BERT model and the For-
est model, with a highlight to the Forest Classifier capability to classifying new data —
generalization. This was show thanks to the 0.93 of ROC AUC.
7.1. Limitations
The human annotation task, which consist in to assign score to the three dimensions, can
be biased because the raters can be influenced to assign violence as high as possible in

order to prove the proposal’s usefulness. Though, we have oriented them to follow the
adapted QOVS as reference. Besides, tweets are short messages which could not provide
enough context to understanding. As consequence, the dimensions (manly planning and
intent) could be rated inadequately during the human annotation task. Other social media
could produce more detailed violent episode descriptions, which will also bring different
technical challenges.
The QOVS scale is intended for analyzing the severity of violence though long
texts, and the use of it in short messages has not been widely tested. While this paper
might present some clues on its possible effectiveness for classification of violence on
short messages, further research is required on a wide range of violent texts through social
media to confirm it.
Another limitation was the lack of samples messages labeled as high, medium and
low violence. This scenario ended up producing an unbalanced dataset, which can make
it difficult for the chosen models to generalize the classification of new messages within
this level of violence.
Finally, the lack of use of K-Fold Cross Validation could have slightly warped
some of the metrics, inserting a extra layer of uncertainty for the future production de-
ployment results of the models. K-Fold Cross Validation will be used in future works.
8. Conclusion
In this paper we presented VISAGE, a computational approach to assess violence using
social media. An adaptation of the QOVS scale and a consolidation of violence level as
being classified into five levels were present as part of VISAGE. After, the visage usage
method was presented as a flow of tasks in the Figure 1. Then we formed four different
datasets of Twitter’ messages. These datasets were used to test and train inference ma-
chine learning systems according to VISAGE’s approach. These systems were used to
perform the experiments. The obtained results have shown that the proposed approach is
feasible.
As future works we intend to: (i) link messages from correlated episodes in other
to be able to infer the episode violence level, (ii) use datasets from other social media,
(iii) use VISAGE on data received on-the-fly, (iv) predict future risk situations given in-
put conditions such as events, economic crisis, religious manifestations and etc, (v) elim-
inate opinion bias in data collection, (vi) combine different machine learning algorithms
in order to further enhance the violence classification, (vii) experiment with other classi-
fication techniques, and the dataset will be made available in the future after the sensible
information, in the form of user IDs, is eliminated. The code used in this study is available
in the repository9.
